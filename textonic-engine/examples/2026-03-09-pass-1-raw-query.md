> "Can you pulverise an extracted wisdom tooth and use the composite material in a kiln to fabricate a new replacement tooth?"

## What came back

### The headline: Someone is already doing almost exactly this.

Liu et al. (2025) published in *Small Structures* a study titled **"3D‐Architected Natural Tooth‐Derived Scaffolds Reinforced by Graphene and Gradient Gyroid"** — their process involves collecting clinically discarded teeth, mechanically milling them into powder, heat-treating the powder (they tested 90°C, 150°C, 500°C, and 700°C — your "kiln" instinct was right, optimal was 500°C), mixing the powder with graphene to create a photocurable slurry, and then 3D printing scaffolds via digital light processing.

Their framing: extracted human teeth are a "renewable resource" rich in hydroxyapatite. Dentin composition is ~70% inorganic (hydroxyapatite), 20% organic (type I collagen), 10% water — compositionally analogous to bone.

**DOI:** [10.1002/sstr.202500498](https://doi.org/10.1002/sstr.202500498) — Open access.

---

### The gap between your idea and what exists

Your idea: pulverise tooth → kiln → **new tooth** (functional replacement).

What the literature does: pulverise tooth → heat-treat → **scaffold for bone repair** (not tooth replacement per se).

The distinction matters. The field has established that tooth-derived hydroxyapatite makes excellent bone graft material. Nobody has yet reconstituted a *functional tooth* from tooth powder — meaning a structure with enamel, dentin, pulp chamber, and periodontal ligament attachment. They're making bone scaffolds from tooth material, not teeth from teeth.

---

### The supporting cast

**Wu et al. (2019)** — "Conditioned Medium of Calcined Tooth Powder Promotes Osteogenic and Odontogenic Differentiation" in *Stem Cells International*. They annealed extracted teeth at 300°C for 30 min (teeth turned black), crushed them with medical pestles, filtered through a 45μm strainer. The resulting calcined tooth powder promoted both bone AND tooth-type cell differentiation via MAPK signaling. This is the closest to your idea — the powder doesn't just make bone, it can trigger tooth-specific regeneration pathways.

**DOI:** [10.1155/2019/4793518](https://doi.org/10.1155/2019/4793518) — Open access.

**Kantoor et al. (2015)** — Studied using extracted teeth as "biological restorations" — literally using fragments of real teeth as filling material. Found mechanical properties hold for about 3-4 weeks in storage media before hardness degrades.

**DOI:** [10.1111/edt.12158](https://doi.org/10.1111/edt.12158)

**Trope (2010)** — Commentary on "biological restoration" using donated tooth fragments as posts and cores, bonded to human roots. Proposed that bovine teeth could potentially be processed at scale by industry for this purpose.

**DOI:** [10.1111/j.1708-8240.2010.00332.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1708-8240.2010.00332.x)

**Wisdom tooth autotransplantation (2014 poster abstract)** — Whole wisdom tooth transplanted to replace a compromised molar. The argument: a transplanted tooth is superior to dental implants because it participates in proprioception, doesn't impede jaw development, and developing roots can induce bone formation.

---

### The materials science reality

The Liu et al. study's thermal analysis is the technical answer to your "kiln" question. When you heat tooth powder:

- **90-150°C**: Drying only, organic components intact
- **500°C**: Optimal — organic matter partially decomposed, hydroxyapatite crystal structure preserved, best printability
- **700°C**: Over-calcined, crystal structure changes, less biocompatible

So yes, a kiln-like process works — but 500°C is the sweet spot, not the higher temperatures you might assume for ceramic firing. The tooth-derived material is already a natural ceramic; you're cleaning it, not creating it.

---

### What your idea adds that doesn't exist yet

The existing literature treats extracted teeth as **raw material for bone scaffolds**. Your idea is more ambitious: tooth-from-tooth as **functional replacement**. The Wu et al. finding that calcined tooth powder triggers odontogenic (tooth-specific) differentiation suggests the biological signals might be there — the powder "remembers" it was a tooth and can tell stem cells to make tooth-like structures. But nobody has closed the loop: powder → scaffold → seed with dental stem cells → grow a functional tooth.

That's the gap. The materials work. The signals work. The assembly is missing.
